There is a plant that grows on the nickel-rich soils of Sulawesi, and if you cut it, dry it, and burn it, the ash that remains can be refined into nickel sulfate that is 99.995% pure—clean enough to go straight into the cathode of an electric-car battery, and almost entirely free of the iron, chromium, and titanium that contaminate conventionally mined ore. The plant is doing the mining. The metallurgy is mostly just harvesting and combustion.

This is not a thought experiment. It is the subject of a 2022 paper in *Science of the Total Environment*, "Farming for battery metals," by Philip Nti Nkrumah, Guillaume Echevarria, Peter Erskine, and Antony van der Ent—the research group that has spent two decades turning this idea into an actual agricultural practice. They call it **agromining**, and the punchline is that a hectare of these "metal crops" can yield 150 to 300 kilograms of nickel a year, worth five to ten thousand dollars, on land too contaminated or too mineral-poor to grow food. They describe it, perfectly, as recovering treasure from trash.

I want to use it to make a point that has very little to do with batteries.

## The plants that mine metals

The world is about to need staggering quantities of battery metals. Demand for nickel, cobalt, lithium, and manganese is projected to rise by factors well above twenty over the coming decades as transport electrifies, and the conventional answers—open-pit mines, artisanal cobalt in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, which supplies roughly 70% of the global supply under conditions nobody wants to defend—carry social and environmental costs that are increasingly hard to ignore.

Agromining offers an unlikely alternative. More than 500 plant species are known to *hyperaccumulate* nickel—to pull it out of the soil and concentrate it in their leaves and stems at levels that would kill an ordinary plant, sometimes to a few percent of their dry weight. Grow them on naturally metal-rich ultramafic soils (Sulawesi alone has more than 15,000 square kilometers of them) or on the tailings left behind by mining, harvest them like any other crop, and the biomass becomes a high-grade "bio-ore." For nickel the economics already roughly pencil out against oil palm. For cobalt and manganese the candidate species exist and the case is building. For lithium, no hyperaccumulator has been found yet—but nobody has systematically looked.

It is a genuinely hopeful technology: low-tech, scalable, suited to rural communities with little infrastructure, and remediating the soil as a side effect. Green technologies, the authors write, from green sources.

## The same biology, pointed the other way

Here is the part that stopped me.

The reason a hyperaccumulator can farm nickel is not exotic. It is the *extreme tail* of a process that happens, to some degree, in every plant we eat. Roots take up the metal dissolved in soil water; the plant moves it upward and stores it in its tissues. A hyperaccumulator does this with industrial enthusiasm. A cacao tree, a rice plant, a head of lettuce does it quietly, in the background, at a thousandth of the intensity—but by the *same* machinery, governed by the *same* variables: soil pH, the parent geology of the rock the soil came from, the redox state of the water, the genetics of the particular cultivar.

This is why the cadmium in chocolate is, overwhelmingly, a soil story. It is why rice is the dominant dietary source of inorganic arsenic—the flooded paddy chemically mobilizes arsenic into a form the plant takes up through its silicon transporters. It is why crops grown on serpentine and ultramafic soils, the very soils prized for nickel agromining, can carry elevated nickel into food. The Heavy Metal Index lays this mechanism out in detail on its [soil-to-plant transfer](https://heavymetalindex.com/supply-chain/soil-to-plant-transfer) page, and the element-by-element behavior on pages like [nickel](https://heavymetalindex.com/metals/nickel). The single most important discipline on those pages is a rule that sounds obvious and is constantly violated: a concentration in soil is *not* a concentration in food. What bridges the two is the transfer factor—and the transfer factor is exactly what the agrominers spent twenty years learning to maximize.

So the agromining literature and the food-contamination literature are not two fields. They are one field, read from two ends. One group is trying to make plants take up as much metal as possible. The rest of us are trying to make them take up as little as possible. They are studying the same dial.

## Why this matters for how we think about contamination

I have argued for a long time—in my [historical analysis of heavy metals in fertilizers](/writing/heavy-metals-fertilizers-historical-analysis), and in the case for [why one-off exposé testing is counterproductive](/writing/public-expose-testing-counterproductive)—that contamination is a supply-chain problem wearing a product-label costume. The cadmium in the baby cereal did not originate in the factory. It came up through the soil, into the grain. If your attention stops at the finished product, you will test symptoms forever and never touch the cause.

Agromining is the most vivid proof of that argument I have ever come across. It demonstrates, commercially, that the soil-to-plant pathway is not a vague risk factor—it is a process precise and powerful enough to *run a farm on*. And that cuts both ways. If we understand uptake well enough to deliberately concentrate metals into a crop, we understand it well enough to deliberately keep them out of one. The bridge between the two is phytoremediation: using accumulating plants to draw metals *out* of contaminated farmland over successive seasons before food crops go back in. The Index treats this as one of the levers in its [agronomic mitigation](https://heavymetalindex.com/mitigation/agronomic) toolkit—sunflower for cadmium, certain ferns for arsenic—and the agromining work is the industrial-strength proof that the lever is real.

This is the systems view I keep returning to. Heavy metals in food are not a fringe scare and not a product defect. They are the visible end of a long biogeochemical chain that runs from bedrock through soil chemistry through plant physiology to the thing on your shelf—the same chain, traced in the same detail, whether you are trying to harvest the metal or avoid it. It is the through-line in why I think [heavy metals are the next major consumer-health issue](/writing/obesity-heavy-metals-sugar-scapegoat), and why I built [the Heavy Metal Index](https://heavymetalindex.com) to map that chain in public, with the citation attached to every link.

A plant that mines nickel and a plant that contaminates dinner are, biologically, the same plant told to do different jobs. Once you see heavy-metal contamination that way—as a transfer process you can read, predict, and intervene in, rather than a mysterious taint that appears in finished goods—the whole problem changes shape. It stops being something to be frightened of and becomes something to be engineered.
